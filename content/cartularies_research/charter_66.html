Source: Eynsham Cartulary, H.E, Salter (ed.)

Charter: 66

Title: De dimidia hida terre in Eston[a].

Approximate date: c.1130–1142 (editorial estimate)

Original Latin

De dimidia hida terre in Eston[a]. Universis sancte ecclesie filiis tam presentibus quam futuris Robertus de Oleio fideles salutes [sic] in domino. Notum sit fraternitati uestre quod dimidiam hidam de terra, quam Rog[erus] de Oleio dedit de feodo meo in Eston[a] ecclesie sancte Marie de Egnesham cum filio suo quem ibi fecit monachum, ego & uxor mea Edith & filius meus Henricus concessimus eidem ecclesie in perpetuum possidendam, liberam & quietam ab omni seruicio preter Danegeld & murdrum tantum, que ad reg[is] coronam pertinent. Huius rei testes sunt, Matheus clericus meus, Walchelinus Waard, Anschetillus de Grai, & Willelmus frater eius & plures alii.

Translation

Robert de Oleio sends greetings in the Lord to all the sons of the Holy Church, both present and future. Let it be known to your brotherhood that the half-hide of land, which Roger de Oleio gave from my fee at Eston to the church of Saint Mary of Eynsham, together with his son, whom he made a monk there, I, together with my wife Edith and my son Henry, have granted to the same church to possess in perpetuity, free and quit from every service except Danegeld and murder, which belong to the Crown. The witnesses to this matter are: Matthew, my clerk, Walchelin Waard, Anschetillus de Grai, William, his brother, and many others.

Notes on Language

Universis sancte ecclesie filiis tam presentibus quam futuris

Literally:

"To all the sons (children) of Holy Church, both present and future." A formal opening addressing the whole Christian community and emphasising that the charter has permanent legal force.

Notum sit fraternitati vestre

Literally:

"Let it be known to your brotherhood." A common legal formula introducing the purpose of the charter.

de feodo meo

Literally:

"From my fee." This indicates that the land formed part of Robert's feudal estate. The phrase is significant because it explains why Robert's confirmation was necessary.

in perpetuum possidendam

Literally:

"To be possessed in perpetuity." Confirms that the church is to hold the land permanently.

liberam et quietam

Literally:

"Free and quit." A standard legal expression indicating that the land is to be held free from the normal feudal services, except where specifically stated.

preter Danegeld et murdrum tantum

Literally:

"Except only Danegeld and murder fine." This is an important legal qualification. The charter exempts the land from ordinary feudal services, but not from certain obligations due to the Crown.

Historical Notes

  • Directly stated by the charter Roger de Oili had previously granted half a hide of land at Eston to St Mary's, Eynsham.
  • The gift was made when Roger's son became a monk at Eynsham.
  • Robert de Oili, together with his wife Edith and his son Henry, confirms the gift.
  • The land is to be held permanently by the Abbey.
  • The land is exempt from all services except Danegeld and murder fine. The charter includes a list of witnesses.

Historical Interpretation

  • This is a confirmation charter, not the original grant.
  • The confirmation suggests that Roger's gift required acknowledgement by the lord of the fee to secure the Abbey's legal title.
  • The inclusion of Edith and Henry alongside Robert suggests that the family wished to reinforce the permanence of the confirmation.
  • The reservation of Danegeld and murder fine illustrates that some royal obligations continued even when land was granted in perpetual alms.

Feudal Relationship

The land is explicitly described as being held: de feodo meo ("from my fee") This establishes that Robert was lord of the fee from which Roger made the original grant.

Historical Significance

Primary significance

This charter records Robert de Oili's confirmation of an earlier grant made by Roger de Oili to St Mary's Abbey, Eynsham. Unlike Charter 64, Robert is not the original donor. His role is to confirm the validity of a grant made from land held within his own fee.

Importance for the D'Oyly Study

    This charter provides:
  • the first explicit confirmation of another member of the D'Oyly family making a grant;
  • the first named reference to Henry, son of Robert; evidence that members of the D'Oyly family held land under Robert's lordship;
  • evidence that entry into monastic life could be associated with gifts of land to a religious house. \

    • Wider Historical Context

    The charter illustrates the interaction between feudal lordship and ecclesiastical patronage in the early twelfth century. It shows how an original donor's gift could be strengthened by the confirmation of the lord who held the superior title to the land.
