Family History

The early D’Oyly family history.

Looking at the medieval period, we see the D’Oyly family living in Oxfordshire, and by investigating the Eynsham Cartulary one of the earliest documentary sources relating to the D’Oyly family in Oxfordshire, the edition looked at was edited by H.E. Salter. Although this set of documents does not provide a complete genealogy, it offers a unique insight into the family’s role as Norman landowners and benefactors of St Mary’s Abbey Eynsham. Although the Cartulary contains many charters, only four relate directly to the branch of the D’Oyly family that forms part of this family history.

Later charters increasingly concern the principal heirs who inherited the D’Oyly estates, whereas the branch leading to our ancestry had already diverged from the main line. These charters illustrate how land was owned, administered and granted to the church during the first half of the twelfth century. The four charters that we are interested in involve Robert D’Oyly who lived from 1065 to 1142, he also married Edith de Greystoke circa 1080 -1090. This marriage is significant because the final charter examined here is issued in Edith's own name. So, taking the charters that I have in date order, and these are purely dates that H. E. Salter proposed during his editing of the Charters.

One thing you will notice is the spelling of the family name, whilst during my commentary I have continued to use the modern variant, medieval spelling was far from standardised, and variations in the family name occur throughout the Cartulary. The spelling has therefore been preserved exactly as it appears in the original Latin text.

Charter 64 dated circa 1130 to 1135.

This charter is the earliest that we have that records an early donation of land by Robert de Oili, together with his wife and sons, to St Mary's Abbey, Eynsham.

This then establishes the D'Oyly family's direct involvement with the Abbey during the early twelfth century. The charter belongs to the period shortly after the Norman settlement of Oxfordshire, when the D'Oyly family were among the principal Norman landholders in the county.

Looking at the details this charter provides:


• one of the earliest documentary references in this section of the Cartulary;
• evidence of family participation in the donation;
• evidence of landholding in both Mullesford and Oxford;
• the beginning of the documented relationship between the D'Oyly family and Eynsham Abbey.

Charter 65 dated Circa 1130 -1135

Charter 65 is already giving us something Charter 64 did not:

It shows Robert D'Oyly exercising lordship over land and tenants in Oxford itself.

The important evidence here is:

• Robert addresses the burgesses of Oxford.
• Robert grants rights to Ruald.
• The land consists of multiple holdings.
• The arrangement includes a continuing annual payment.

This is a very different view of Robert from Charter 64, where he appeared primarily as a benefactor of Eynsham Abbey.

Here we see Robert as a landholder and administrator.

Charter 66 dated circa 1130 – 1142

it should be noted that Robert died in 1142.

This charter records Robert de Oili's confirmation of an earlier grant made by Roger de Oili to St Mary's Abbey, Eynsham.
Unlike Charter 64, Robert is not the original donor. His role is to confirm the validity of a grant made from land held within his own fee.

This particular charter provides:
• the first explicit confirmation of another member of the D'Oyly family making a grant;
• the first named reference to Henry, son of Robert;
• evidence that members of the D'Oyly family held land under Robert's lordship;
• evidence that entry into monastic life could be associated with gifts of land to a religious house.

Charter 67 dated before Roberts death in 1142

This charter is issued solely in Edith's name.

It demonstrates that Edith exercised recognised authority in relation to property associated with Cleydon. The charter is also significant because it records the transfer of a dependent tenant and his household rather than the transfer of land itself. It therefore provides evidence for another aspect of estate administration within the D'Oilly lordship.

When read alongside Salter's editorial note, the charter suggests that Claydon held a particular significance within Edith's own property interests, although that conclusion depends upon evidence external to the charter.


Conclusion:

Taken together, these charters provide the earliest documentary evidence for the D'Oyly family's relationship with Eynsham Abbey. They show the family acting not only as benefactors to the Church but also as landholders, administrators and feudal lords. While the Cartulary does not provide a complete genealogy, it offers a valuable glimpse into the world in which the early generations of the family lived within Norman Oxfordshire and the legal mechanisms by which land was granted, confirmed and inherited.
